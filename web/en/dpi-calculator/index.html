What are pixels, DPI and PPI?

A pixel is the smallest building block of a digital image, a tiny square holding one color. A photo described as 3000 × 2000 pixels has 3000 columns and 2000 rows, six million dots in total, or six megapixels. DPI stands for dots per inch and describes how tightly a printer packs ink dots into one inch of paper. PPI stands for pixels per inch and describes the same idea for images and screens. Print professionals care about the difference, but for converting a pixel count into a physical size the two behave identically, so this page says DPI when talking about paper and PPI when talking about displays.

The most common misunderstanding is that DPI changes the number of pixels in a file. It does not. A 3000 × 2000 image is always six megapixels. DPI only decides how widely those pixels are spread when the image is printed. Pack them tightly and you get a small, crisp print; spread them out and you get a large print that looks softer. The DPI value saved in a file is just a suggested print density, and editing that label with resampling switched off does not touch a single pixel.

The formulas behind the numbers

Everything on this page comes from three short calculations. Divide pixels by DPI to get inches, and multiply inches by 2.54 for centimeters. Go the other way by dividing a length in inches by one and multiplying by DPI. For screens, take the diagonal of the pixel grid with the Pythagorean theorem and divide by the diagonal size in inches.

  • Pixels to inches: inches = pixels ÷ DPI. Example: 3000 pixels at 300 DPI is 10 inches, or 25.4 cm.
  • Inches to pixels: pixels = inches × DPI. Example: 8 inches at 300 DPI needs 2400 pixels.
  • Screen PPI: PPI = √(width² + height²) ÷ diagonal. Example: a 27-inch 1920 × 1080 monitor has a pixel diagonal of 2202.9, so 2202.9 ÷ 27 = 81.59 PPI.

How many DPI do you really need?

It depends on how closely people will look at the result. For photographs, brochures, business cards and books, which are held at arm's length or closer, 300 DPI is the usual target because the eye can no longer pick out individual dots. For posters, banners and large wall prints that are viewed from a meter or more, 150 DPI often looks perfectly good, and billboards viewed from the road can drop far lower. Photo labs and online print services usually publish a minimum resolution for each product, so check their upload page if you can.

Large prints are a good illustration of why pixel count matters more than any DPI tag. A modern 12-megapixel phone photo is about 4000 × 3000 pixels. At 300 DPI that is a bit over 13 inches wide, enough for a very sharp 8×10. Stretch the same file to a 24×36 poster and the density falls to about 111 DPI, which is acceptable from across the room but visibly soft up close. Cropping makes things worse, because you throw away pixels you need.

Practical examples

Imagine you have a 1600 × 1200 pixel image from an old digital camera and you want to hang it as a 5×7 print. Enter the dimensions in the quick answer box and you will see that it reaches roughly 229 DPI when filling a 5×7 frame by cropping, which is good, but only about 133 DPI when stretched to 8×10, which is soft. That single check can save you a wasted print order.

Another common case is preparing a graphic for a specific print size. A designer asked to deliver a US Letter flyer at 300 DPI needs a canvas of 2550 × 3300 pixels. Switch to the second mode, leave the unit on inches, type 8.5 and 11, and the answer appears instantly. If the printer wants half an inch of bleed on each side, enter 9.5 and 12 instead and you will get 2850 × 3600 pixels.

Screen density helps when you buy a monitor or design for several devices. A 24-inch Full HD monitor is about 92 PPI, a 27-inch 4K monitor is about 163 PPI, and a recent phone with a 6.1-inch display and a 2556 × 1179 panel is above 450 PPI. Knowing these numbers explains why text that looks smooth on a phone can look jagged on an older monitor, and why designers export images at two or three times their displayed size for high-density screens.

Tips for sharper prints

  • Start from the original file whenever possible. Photos sent through messaging apps are often shrunk to about 1600 pixels on the long side, which is too small for anything larger than a 5×7.
  • Crop before you calculate. If you will remove a quarter of the frame, use the cropped pixel dimensions, because that is what the printer will actually receive.
  • Do not enlarge by raising the DPI number in an editor with resampling turned on. It adds guessed pixels, not detail, and can create a plastic look.
  • Keep text and logos as vector artwork when you can. Vector shapes stay sharp at any size and do not depend on DPI at all.
  • Leave a safe margin. Print shops trim paper slightly differently from job to job, so keep important content at least a quarter inch from the edge.

Frequently asked questions

How many inches is 3000 pixels at 300 DPI?

Exactly 10 inches, which is 25.4 centimeters. Divide the pixel count by the DPI to get inches, then multiply by 2.54 for centimeters. At 150 DPI the same 3000 pixels stretch to 20 inches, but the print looks softer.

How many pixels do I need for an 8x10 print?

At 300 DPI you need 2400 × 3000 pixels, and at 150 DPI you need 1200 × 1500 pixels. If your photo has a different shape, such as 3:2, it will be cropped to fit unless you accept white borders.

Is DPI the same as PPI?

Strictly, no. DPI counts printer dots per inch and PPI counts image or screen pixels per inch. In everyday use the words are swapped, and the arithmetic for converting pixels to inches is the same for both.

Is 150 DPI enough for printing?

For posters, flyers and wall prints viewed from a meter or more, usually yes. For handheld photos, books and brochures, aim for 300 DPI. Ask your print shop if the job is important, because paper and printer type change the answer.

Can I raise the DPI of a low-resolution photo?

Changing the DPI label does not add pixels. If you enlarge the print the pixels spread out and the picture gets blurrier. Upscaling software can invent pixels but cannot recover real detail, so the best fix is a higher-resolution original.

What is the PPI of a 27-inch Full HD monitor?

About 81.59 PPI. The same resolution on a 24-inch panel is about 91.79 PPI, so smaller screens look sharper at equal resolution. Switch to the Screen PPI mode to try your own display.

How do I find the DPI stored in an image file?

This calculator does not read files. On Windows, right-click the file, choose Properties and open the Details tab. On a Mac, open the image in Preview and choose Tools, then Adjust Size. If you need to change the pixel size itself, use the Resize Image tool.

Should I type commas or dots in the number fields?

Use a dot for decimals (25.4) and, if you like, commas for thousands (3,000). Do not add letters or units inside the box; choose the unit from the list instead.

Are my values sent anywhere?

No. The calculation runs in your browser. Nothing is uploaded, stored or tracked, and once the page has loaded it also works offline.
