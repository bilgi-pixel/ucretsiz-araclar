How to use the square root calculator

  1. Choose Take a root to find square roots, cube roots and higher roots, or Raise to a power for exponents.
  2. Type your number. For a root, leave the degree at 2 for a square root, or enter 3 for a cube root, 4 for a fourth root and so on up to 100.
  3. Press Calculate root (or Calculate power). The headline result appears first, for example √72 = 8.4852813742.
  4. Check the Simplified row for the radical form, 6√2 in this case, and the neighbor rows to see which perfect squares or cubes surround your number.
  5. Copy the result with one click, or press Clear to start over.

What is a square root?

The square root of a number is a value that gives the original number when multiplied by itself. Because 8 × 8 = 64, the square root of 64 is 8. Every positive number has two square roots, one positive and one negative, since (−8) × (−8) is also 64. By convention, the radical sign √ means the positive, or principal, root, so √64 = 8. The number under the radical is called the radicand.

Numbers like 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36 and 49 are perfect squares: their roots are whole numbers. Most numbers are not. The square root of 2 is about 1.41421356 and it never ends or repeats, which makes it an irrational number. A calculator can only show an approximation of such a root, but a radical such as √2 is an exact value, and the tool shows both.

How to simplify a radical

Simplifying means pulling every perfect square factor out from under the radical sign. To simplify √72, find the largest perfect square that divides 72. That is 36, because 72 = 36 × 2. Then √72 = √36 × √2 = 6√2. The same idea works for higher roots: for a cube root you pull out perfect cubes, so ³√54 = ³√(27 × 2) = 3³√2.

The calculator finds this automatically by breaking the number into its prime factors and moving each group of equal primes outside. Algebra and geometry teachers usually expect the simplified form on tests, for instance when using the Pythagorean theorem. A right triangle with legs 6 and 6 has a hypotenuse of √72, which you would write as 6√2.

Cube roots, nth roots and negative numbers

A cube root asks which number multiplied by itself three times gives the original. Since 3 × 3 × 3 = 27, the cube root of 27 is 3. Unlike square roots, cube roots work for negative numbers: the cube root of −27 is −3. In general, odd roots of negative numbers are real, while even roots are not. There is no real number whose square is −9. In the complex number system the answer is 3i, where i is defined by i² = −1, and the tool mentions this in its message instead of returning a confusing error.

Roots are also exponents in disguise. The square root of x equals x raised to the power 1/2, and the cube root equals x to the 1/3. That is why the exponent mode accepts decimal powers. Try 16 to the power 0.5 and you will get 4, the same as √16. A negative exponent flips the number: 2 to the power −3 is 1/8, or 0.125.

Exact powers for big integers

When you raise a whole number to a whole-number exponent, the tool computes the exact integer using arbitrary-precision arithmetic instead of a rounded decimal. 2 to the power 64 comes out as 18,446,744,073,709,551,616 with every digit correct, along with a digit count. For results that cannot be written as an integer, such as 2 to the power 0.5, the tool shows up to ten decimal places, and very large or very small results switch to scientific notation.

Where square roots show up in real life

Roots are everywhere in practical work. Carpenters use the Pythagorean theorem to check that a frame is square: a 3-4-5 triangle has a hypotenuse of √(9 + 16) = 5. Photographers and designers compute diagonal lengths from width and height. Statisticians take a square root of the variance to obtain the standard deviation. Programmers use roots in distance formulas for games and maps. If you work with dimensions, the Aspect Ratio Calculator and the Unit Converter pair well with this tool.

Accuracy and limits

Decimal results are computed with standard double-precision numbers, which are accurate to about 15 significant digits. Exact roots of perfect powers are detected and shown without rounding noise. Simplified radical form is available for whole numbers up to one trillion. The root degree can be any whole number from 2 to 100. These limits keep the page responsive on any device.

Frequently asked questions

What is the square root of 2?

It is approximately 1.41421356. The number is irrational, so its decimal expansion never ends or repeats. The exact value is written as the radical sign followed by 2.

How do I simplify the square root of 72?

Factor out the largest perfect square. 72 = 36 x 2, so the square root of 72 equals the square root of 36 times the square root of 2, which is 6 times the square root of 2 (about 8.4853).

What is a perfect square?

A perfect square is a whole number that is the square of another whole number, such as 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36 and 49. The tool tells you whether your number is one and shows the nearest perfect squares on either side.

Can you take the square root of a negative number?

Not within the real numbers, because no real number squared is negative. In complex numbers the square root of -9 is 3i. The calculator explains this instead of returning a wrong value. Odd roots, such as the cube root of -8, do work and give -2.

How do I find a cube root or a fourth root?

Set the root degree to 3 for a cube root, 4 for a fourth root and so on. The tool supports degrees from 2 to 100 and shows exact whole-number results when the input is a perfect power.

Is a square root the same as raising to the power of 0.5?

Yes. The square root of x equals x to the power 0.5, and the cube root equals x to the power 1/3. You can confirm this in the exponent mode with a decimal such as 0.5.

How precise are the results?

Decimal values are accurate to roughly 15 significant digits, and exact roots and integer powers are detected without rounding error. Integer powers are computed with big-integer math, so all digits are exact.

What is the square root of 0 and of 1?

The square root of 0 is 0, and the square root of 1 is 1. Both are perfect squares.

Are my numbers uploaded anywhere?

No. Every calculation happens in your browser. Nothing is sent, stored or tracked.
