    How to use the resistor color code calculator

    1. Hold the resistor so the tolerance band (usually gold or silver, set slightly apart from the others) is on the right. Read the bands from left to right.
    2. Under Colors to value, choose 4, 5 or 6 bands, then select the color of each band from the drop-down lists. The default shows a 4.7 kΩ resistor so you can see how it works.
    3. Press Find the value. You get the resistance in ohms, the tolerance, the lowest and highest value the part may measure, and a drawing of the resistor with a list of the colors.
    4. To go the other way, choose Value to colors, type a value such as 220, 4.7k, 4k7 or 1M, pick a tolerance and press Find the colors.
    5. Copy the result for your schematic or parts list.

    The resistor color chart

    Resistors are too small to print numbers on, so manufacturers wrap them in colored bands following the international standard IEC 60062. Each color stands for a digit, a multiplier or a tolerance. Memorize this table and you can read almost any through-hole resistor:

    ColorDigitMultiplierTolerance
    Black0×1–
    Brown1×10±1%
    Red2×100±2%
    Orange3×1k–
    Yellow4×10k–
    Green5×100k±0.5%
    Blue6×1M±0.25%
    Violet7×10M±0.1%
    Grey8×100M±0.05%
    White9×1G–
    Gold–×0.1±5%
    Silver–×0.01±10%

    Hobbyists often remember the order with a mnemonic, for example Big Boys Race Our Young Girls But Violet Generally Wins. The wording does not matter as long as it fixes the sequence black, brown, red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet, grey, white in your head.

    Reading 4, 5 and 6 band resistors

    4-band resistors are the most common in hobby kits. The first two bands are digits, the third is the multiplier and the fourth is the tolerance. Yellow, violet, red, gold reads as 4, 7, ×100 = 4,700 ohms, or 4.7 kΩ, with a ±5% tolerance. That means the real resistance may lie between 4.465 kΩ and 4.935 kΩ.

    5-band resistors are precision parts. They use three digits, a multiplier and a tolerance, so brown, black, black, red, brown reads as 1, 0, 0, ×100 = 10,000 ohms, or 10 kΩ, ±1%. Precision film resistors with 1% or better tolerance almost always use five bands.

    6-band resistors add a sixth band for the temperature coefficient, in parts per million per degree Celsius. Brown means 100 ppm/°C, red 50, orange 15, yellow 25, blue 10 and violet 5. A lower number means the value drifts less when the part heats up, which matters in measurement and audio circuits.

    What tolerance and range mean

    No resistor is perfect. A 1 kΩ part with ±5% tolerance is guaranteed to measure somewhere between 950 and 1,050 ohms. The calculator shows that interval as the actual value range. For an LED current-limiting resistor or a pull-up on a microcontroller input, 5% is perfectly fine. For voltage dividers in sensor circuits, op-amp gain settings and filters, 1% or better keeps your design predictable. If you are checking a part with a multimeter, make sure the measured value falls inside the range, and measure it out of the circuit, since other components in parallel can change the reading.

    Standard values: E12 and E24 series

    Manufacturers do not make every possible value. Resistors follow preferred-number series. The E12 series, common for 10% and 5% parts, has twelve values per decade: 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68 and 82. The E24 series, for 5% and 2% parts, adds twelve more such as 11, 13, 16, 20, 24, 30, 36, 43, 51, 62, 75 and 91. The E96 series, with 1% tolerance, has 96 values per decade and requires three digits, so it is printed with five bands. If you type a value that needs more digits than the band count allows, such as 4,750 ohms on a 4-band part, the tool explains that you should switch to five bands.

    Practical examples

    • LED resistor. A red LED on a 5 V supply typically needs about 220 ohms to 330 ohms. Enter 220 to find red, red, brown, gold.
    • Pull-up resistor. 10 kΩ is the classic value: brown, black, orange, gold on a 4-band part.
    • Voltage divider. Two 10 kΩ resistors halve a voltage. Check that both parts have the same tolerance so the ratio stays accurate.
    • Unknown part from a junk box. Pick the colors, read the value, and confirm with a multimeter before using it.

    Tips for reading the bands correctly

    • Start from the end with bands closer together. The tolerance band is often separated by a wider gap, and gold or silver never appears as the first digit.
    • Old, burnt or dirty resistors can change color. Brown and red or orange and red can look alike, so use good light.
    • Surface-mount resistors use printed numbers, not bands. Those are outside the scope of this tool.
    • Zero-ohm links are marked with a single black band and are not covered here.
    • Power rating is not encoded in the bands. Check the size or the datasheet for the wattage.

    Accuracy and limits

    The tool follows IEC 60062 for 4, 5 and 6 band axial resistors. Colors on real parts vary between manufacturers and fade with age, so always confirm important values with a meter. The temperature coefficient is displayed exactly as encoded but different manufacturers may use slightly different conventions for the sixth band.

    Frequently asked questions

    How do I read a 4-band resistor?

    Read from the end without the gap. The first two bands are digits, the third is a multiplier and the fourth is tolerance. Yellow, violet, red, gold means 47 × 100 = 4,700 ohms (4.7 kΩ) with ±5% tolerance.

    What is the difference between 4-band and 5-band resistors?

    A 5-band resistor has a third digit for higher precision, so values like 4.75 kΩ can be coded. Most 5-band parts are 1% or better, while 4-band parts are typically 5% or 10%.

    What does the sixth band on a 6-band resistor mean?

    It is the temperature coefficient, which says how much the resistance drifts per degree Celsius, in parts per million. Brown is 100 ppm/°C, red 50, orange 15, yellow 25, blue 10 and violet 5.

    Which end do I start reading from?

    Start from the side where the bands are closer together; the tolerance band, often gold or silver, sits slightly apart. A gold or silver band can never be the first digit. If in doubt, try both directions and see which gives a standard value.

    How do I write 4.7 kΩ in color bands?

    Switch to Value to colors, type 4.7k or 4k7, choose a tolerance and press Find the colors. For a 4-band resistor with gold tolerance, you get yellow, violet, red, gold.

    What are gold and silver bands for?

    In the tolerance position gold means ±5% and silver ±10%. In the multiplier position gold means ×0.1 and silver ×0.01, which allows values below 10 ohms such as 4.7 ohms (yellow, violet, gold).

    Why does the tool say my value cannot be shown with 4 bands?

    A 4-band resistor only carries two significant digits, so 4,750 ohms (three digits) needs a 5-band part. Pick the 5-band option or choose a standard E12 or E24 value.

    Does the tolerance range matter in practice?

    Yes. A 10 kΩ resistor with ±5% can measure anywhere from 9.5 kΩ to 10.5 kΩ. Use 1% parts where accuracy matters, such as dividers, filters and precision references.
