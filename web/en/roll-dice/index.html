    How to use the dice roller

    1. Choose how many dice you want to throw, from one to six. Two is the default because most board games use a pair.
    2. Pick the type of die. The standard six-sided die (d6) is drawn with pips; the d4, d8, d10, d12, d20 and d100 show the number that came up.
    3. Optional: type a modifier such as +3 or -1. The modifier is added to the sum of the dice, which is how most tabletop role-playing games work.
    4. Press Roll the dice. The faces, the total and the full calculation appear immediately and the roll is added to the history.
    5. Roll as often as you like. The page keeps the last 20 rolls and the average total, so you can see how the results behave over time.

    A fair online dice roller that runs on your device

    Everyone has lost a die under the sofa or found that the one in the game box went missing years ago. This page gives you a replacement that works on a phone, tablet or computer, with no download and no sign-up. It is useful for family board games, tabletop role-playing sessions, classroom probability lessons, quick tie-breaks and any moment where you need a random number between one and the number of sides.

    Each die is rolled with the random number generator built into your browser, the same source of randomness used for security features. The tool uses a method that removes tiny statistical biases, so every face of a die is equally likely. Nothing about your rolls is sent anywhere. The history exists only on the page and disappears when you close it.

    Dice notation such as 2d6+3

    Role-playing guides write rolls as dice notation. The first number is how many dice to roll, the letter d means die, the number after it is the number of sides, and anything after that is a modifier. So 3d8 means roll three eight-sided dice and add them, while 1d20+5 means roll one twenty-sided die and add five. In this tool you set the count and type with the menus and the modifier in the optional box. The history lists each roll in that notation so you can read it at a glance.

    The odds of rolling two dice

    With one fair die every result from one to six has the same chance, one in six. With two dice the totals are no longer equally likely, because some totals can be made in more ways than others. There are 36 equally likely combinations of two six-sided dice, and seven can be made in six different ways, so it comes up one time in six on average. Two and twelve can each be made in only one way and appear only once in 36 rolls.

    Good ways to use the roller

    • Board and party games: keep playing when the dice are missing, or let each player roll on their own phone.
    • Role-playing games: roll attack, damage and skill checks without carrying a dice bag. The d20 and modifier boxes are all you need for most checks.
    • Teaching probability: have students predict the total of two dice, roll fifty times and compare their results with the table above.
    • Making decisions: assign options to numbers and roll. A d6 can choose between six restaurants or six chores.

    Fair play, accessibility and limits

    The tool is built for entertainment, teaching and everyday decisions. It is not intended for gambling for money, and it makes no claim about being certified for regulated games. Dice results are announced to screen readers when you roll, all controls work with the keyboard and the shaking animation switches off automatically if your device is set to reduce motion.

    Remember that every roll is independent. After three sixes in a row, the next roll is still a one-in-six chance of a six; the dice have no memory. This is called the gambler's fallacy, and it is a good reason to be careful when you see patterns in a short run of results.

    Roll a die to decide

    A single die is also a decision tool. Number your options from one to six, roll and go with the result. If you have more than six choices, switch to a d8, d10, d12 or d20 and number the list accordingly, or use the d100 for percentages and long lists. Families use a d6 to pick who loads the dishwasher, teams use a d20 to choose the order of presentations, and game masters use a d100 to consult random tables. Because the tool shows the full calculation, you can also settle arguments about whether a roll was fair: the faces and total are right there on screen.

    Private by design

    The page contains no tracking code and needs no account. Your rolls, your history and the averages are kept in your browser tab only, and clearing the history or closing the page removes them completely.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is this online dice roller really random?

    Yes. It uses the cryptographic random number generator built into your browser and removes modulo bias, so every face has exactly the same chance. Past results never influence future rolls.

    How do I roll two dice?

    Set Number of dice to 2, keep the die type on d6 and press Roll the dice. The faces, their sum and the calculation appear straight away.

    Can I roll a d20 with a modifier?

    Yes. Choose d20, set the number of dice to 1 and type a modifier such as +5 in the optional box. The total shows the die plus the modifier, for example 14 + 5 = 19.

    What is the average of two six-sided dice?

    The average total of two fair six-sided dice is 7. Seven is also the most likely single total, coming up one time in six, while 2 and 12 appear only one time in 36.

    What does 3d6 mean?

    It means roll three six-sided dice and add the results. In this tool, set the number of dice to 3 and the die type to d6.

    Why are only d6 dice drawn with dots?

    The classic six-sided die is the one most people know, so it is drawn with pips. Other dice show a number because their faces would be hard to read as dots.

    How many rolls does the history keep?

    The tool keeps the last 20 rolls and a running average of the total for the current visit. Clear history resets both. Nothing is saved after you close the page.

    Can I use it offline?

    Yes. After the page has loaded once it works without a connection, and you can install it as an app from your browser menu.

    Are the results sent to a server?

    No. The random numbers are generated and displayed entirely in your browser. No rolls are uploaded, stored or tracked.
