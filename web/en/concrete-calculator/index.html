  1. Choose the shape. Pick Slab, wall or footing for anything rectangular, such as a patio, driveway, shed base, sidewalk or footing trench. Pick Round column or tube for sonotube footings, deck piers and fence post holes.

How many cubic yards of concrete do I need?

Concrete in the United States is sold by the cubic yard, which is a cube three feet on each side. That equals 27 cubic feet, or about 0.76 cubic meters. The math behind every concrete estimate is the same: multiply length by width by thickness, with everything in the same unit, then convert to cubic yards. A 12 by 10 foot patio that is 4 inches thick has a volume of 12 × 10 × (4 ÷ 12) = 40 cubic feet, which is 1.48 cubic yards. Add 10 percent for waste and you get about 1.63 cubic yards, so you would order 1.75 yards from a ready-mix plant.

Doing this by hand is easy until you mix feet and inches, change units halfway, or need to compare bags with a truck delivery. The calculator handles the conversions, rounds the delivery up the way suppliers do, and shows the bag counts side by side so you can decide whether to mix by hand or call the truck.

Ready-mix or bagged concrete?

As a rough rule, a job that needs less than about one cubic yard is a candidate for bagged mix, and anything over that is usually easier and often cheaper with a truck. One cubic yard is around 45 bags of 80 pound mix, 60 bags of 60 pound mix or 90 bags of 40 pound mix. Mixing 45 bags by hand or in a small drum mixer is hard work, and concrete starts to set, so a large pour that is mixed slowly can end up with cold joints and a weak surface.

Ready-mix plants often add a short-load or minimum-order fee for small deliveries. The threshold varies by company and region, and some plants charge for waiting time if the truck sits on site too long. When your estimate is close to the minimum, it can make sense to widen the pour slightly, add a footing or a pad you were planning for later, or share a delivery with a neighbor. Call two or three plants for prices, because the cost per yard varies a lot between metro areas.

Why add extra for waste?

The exact geometric volume is almost never enough. Sub-grades are rarely perfectly level, forms bow slightly under the weight of wet concrete, some mix stays in the truck chute and the wheelbarrow, and a little always spills. A 5 to 10 percent allowance is normal; use the lower end for a precise, well-prepared form on flat ground and the higher end when the base is uneven, when you are digging a trench footing in soil or when you cannot afford to come up short. Running out in the middle of a pour is far more expensive than ordering a quarter yard extra, because a second delivery usually triggers a minimum charge and the first batch will already have started to set.

Planning the pour

Prepare the base before the truck arrives. Excavate to the right depth, compact the soil, add and compact a few inches of gravel for slabs, set the forms level with a slight slope away from buildings for drainage, and add rebar or wire mesh if your plan calls for it. Check the weather: concrete should not be placed on frozen ground, and very hot or windy days make it dry too fast. Have enough helpers, a screed board, a bull float and an edger ready, because you have a limited window to finish the surface. Protect the slab as it cures; it reaches most of its strength in about a week and its design strength in roughly 28 days.

For small projects with bags, have a sturdy mixing container, a hoe or a mixer, and clean water. Add water gradually and follow the directions on the bag, because too much water makes concrete weaker and more likely to crack. Accuracy and limits of an online estimate

This tool calculates geometric volume and applies the waste percentage you choose. The bag counts use common published yields, but products differ, so read the label of the exact bag you buy. Real jobs may have sloped bottoms, thickened edges, steps, curves or voids that are not captured by a simple box or cylinder. For those, split the work into several simple shapes, calculate each one, and add the results by running the tool more than once. When money or safety is at stake, ask your supplier to double-check the quantity before you order.

Frequently asked questions

How many bags of concrete make one cubic yard?

With typical yields, one cubic yard needs about 45 bags of 80 pound mix, 60 bags of 60 pound mix or 90 bags of 40 pound mix. Yields differ slightly between brands, so check the label and round up.

How much does a cubic yard of concrete cover?

One cubic yard covers about 81 square feet at 4 inches thick, 65 square feet at 5 inches thick or 54 square feet at 6 inches thick. Those figures are before any waste allowance.

What is the formula for cubic yards of concrete?

Multiply length in feet by width in feet by thickness in feet, which is inches divided by 12, and then divide the result by 27 to convert cubic feet to cubic yards. For round columns use the area of the circle, pi times the radius squared, instead of length times width.

How much extra concrete should I order?

Most contractors add 5 to 10 percent. Use about 5 percent for a clean, level form and 10 percent for uneven ground, trench footings or when you cannot risk running short. The calculator uses 10 percent by default and lets you change it.

Is it cheaper to mix concrete myself?

For very small jobs, bagged mix is convenient and avoids delivery minimums. For anything near a cubic yard or more, ready-mix is often similar in cost once you count your time, tool rental and the risk of an uneven mix, and the quality is usually better.

Why is the ready-mix order rounded up?

Plants commonly sell concrete in quarter-yard or half-yard steps and expect you to order a little over, never under. The tool rounds your total up to the next quarter yard so the number matches how suppliers talk about orders.

Can I use this calculator with metric measurements?

Yes. Switch the units to meters and centimeters, enter your measurements and the tool converts everything. Results are shown in cubic yards, cubic feet and cubic meters at the same time.

Does the calculator work for stairs, curved paths or irregular shapes?

Not directly. Break the shape into rectangles and cylinders, calculate each part separately and add the volumes. For a curved path, an average width times the center-line length gives a good estimate.

Are my measurements sent to a server?

No. All calculations run in your browser. Nothing is uploaded, stored or tracked, and the page keeps working offline once it has loaded.
