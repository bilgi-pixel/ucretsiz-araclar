    How to use the coin flipper

    1. Decide what heads and what tails will mean before you flip. For example, heads means you pick the movie and tails means your friend picks.
    2. Choose how many coins to flip. One coin settles a yes-or-no decision; two to ten coins are useful for probability experiments and games.
    3. Press Flip the coin. The coin spins and lands on heads or tails, and the result is announced and shown in large text.
    4. Keep flipping as long as you like. The page counts heads, tails, total flips and your current streak, and keeps a list of the last 20 flips.
    5. Press Reset counts to start a new round.

    Flip a coin online, fairly and instantly

    Flipping a coin is the oldest tie-breaker there is. It is quick, everybody understands it and it feels fair because nobody controls the outcome. The trouble is that nobody carries coins anymore. This page gives you a digital coin that works on any phone, tablet or computer. There is nothing to install and no account to create, and the flip happens on your device, so it also works without a connection once the page has loaded.

    Each flip uses the random number generator built into your browser, which is designed for security uses and is much better than the simple pseudo-random functions many web toys rely on. Heads and tails each have exactly a fifty percent chance on every flip. The previous flip has no effect on the next one.

    When people flip a coin

    • Settling small disputes: who goes first, who pays for coffee, which route to take, which show to watch.
    • Sports: a coin toss decides who kicks off, who chooses ends of the field, or who bats first in many amateur matches. Teams and clubs can use this page when the referee has forgotten the coin.
    • Classrooms: teachers use coin flips to introduce probability, expected value and the idea that small samples are noisy.
    • Games and activities: simple heads-or-tails games, charades turns and party challenges.
    • Gut-check decisions: if you notice yourself hoping for a particular side while the coin is in the air, you have learned what you wanted.

    The counts also demonstrate the law of large numbers. After ten flips you might see seven heads and three tails, which looks lopsided. After a thousand flips the share of heads will usually be very close to 50 percent, even though the absolute difference between heads and tails can still be a few dozen. Try flipping ten coins at once a few dozen times and watch how the number of heads clusters around five.

    Is a real coin really fifty-fifty?

    For practical purposes yes, but physical coins are not perfect. A study by mathematicians and statisticians published in 2007 analyzed the physics of coin tosses and found that a coin tends to land the same way up that it started with slightly more often than half the time, around 51 percent. That bias is tiny and irrelevant for settling who washes the dishes, and a digital flip such as this one has no starting side to bias it. If a decision is very important, do not leave it to chance; use the coin for tie-breakers, not for matters that deserve careful thought.

    Tips for fair decisions

    Say out loud which side means what before you flip, and agree to accept the result. For a best-of-three or best-of-five series, keep flipping and watch the heads and tails counters. If you need more than two outcomes, try our Roll Dice page or set a range in the Random Number Generator. A six-sided die can pick between six options, and a number range can choose a winner from a list of names numbered one to however many you like.

    The coin respects your device settings. If you have asked your phone or computer to reduce motion, the spin animation is skipped and the result appears immediately. Results are written in text as well as shown on the coin, so screen readers announce them as soon as the flip finishes.

    Coin flips in probability lessons

    Teachers like coins because the outcome space is tiny and everyone can predict it. A good classroom exercise is to ask students to write down a made-up sequence of twenty flips, then compare it with a real sequence produced by this page. Invented sequences usually contain too few long streaks and switch sides too often, while a genuine random sequence looks streakier than people expect. Another idea is to flip ten coins at once fifty times and draw a bar chart of the number of heads in each round. The chart takes the familiar bell shape, with five heads as the tallest bar and zero or ten heads almost never seen. It is a vivid, hands-on way to introduce the binomial distribution without any formulas.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is the online coin flip fair?

    Yes. Each flip uses the cryptographic random number generator in your browser, so heads and tails each have a 50 percent chance and earlier flips never influence later ones.

    Can I flip more than one coin at a time?

    Yes. Choose 1, 2, 3, 5 or 10 coins. For several coins the tool shows each coin and tells you how many heads and tails you got.

    What is the chance of getting heads five times in a row?

    It is one half multiplied by itself five times, which is about 3.1 percent or roughly one in 32. Ten in a row is about one in 1,024.

    Why do I get streaks of the same side?

    Random sequences contain streaks more often than most people expect. A run of four or five heads is entirely normal in a long series. Each flip is still 50-50 regardless of the streak.

    Is a coin toss really 50-50?

    A fair digital flip is. A physical coin may land on its starting side slightly more often, about 51 percent according to research, but this tool has no starting side so it has no such bias.

    Does the page work without internet?

    Yes. After it has loaded once, the coin works offline and can be installed as an app from your browser menu.

    What do the counters show?

    They show the number of heads, the number of tails, the total flips since the last reset and your current streak of the same side. Reset counts clears them.

    Can I use it to make important decisions?

    It is best for small, low-stakes choices and tie-breakers. For big decisions, a coin can help you notice your own preference, but it should not replace careful thinking or professional advice.

    Is anything stored or sent to a server?

    No. The flips happen in your browser and nothing is uploaded or stored. Closing the page clears the counts and history.
