How to use it

  1. Type the amount you start with in "Starting amount". Commas as thousands separators are fine, so 10,000 and 10000 give the same result.
  2. Enter the annual interest rate as a percentage. For 5% just type 5; decimals such as 4.25 are accepted.
  3. Enter the period in years. Use 1.5 for eighteen months or 0.25 for three months; the calculation runs month by month, so fractional periods are handled correctly.
  4. Choose how often interest is compounded: yearly, every six months, quarterly, monthly or daily. If you are unsure, monthly is the most common choice for savings accounts.
  5. If you add money every month, enter that amount as the monthly contribution, then press "Calculate compound interest". The result shows your final balance, total deposits, interest earned and a table with the balance at the end of each year.

What is compound interest?

Compound interest means the interest you earn is added to your balance and then earns interest itself. With simple interest you are paid only on the original amount; with compound interest the base grows every period. Over a short time the difference looks small, but over many years the growth speeds up noticeably. That is why compounding is the engine of long-term saving, and also why unpaid credit card balances can grow so quickly.

The classic formula is A = P × (1 + r / n)n × t, where P is the starting amount, r is the annual rate as a decimal (5% is 0.05), n is the number of times interest is added per year and t is the number of years. For example, 10,000 at 5% compounded yearly for 10 years becomes 10,000 × 1.0510 ≈ 16,288.95. With monthly compounding the result is slightly higher because interest joins the balance every month.

Adding monthly contributions

Most people do not invest one large sum; they set aside part of every paycheck. This calculator adds your monthly contribution at the end of each month and lets it earn interest from the following month onward. To keep things consistent whatever the compounding frequency, it first converts the annual rate into an equivalent monthly rate: monthly rate = (1 + r / n)n / 12 − 1. The table then shows, for every year, how much you have put in, how much interest has built up and the resulting balance, so you can watch the interest share of your savings grow over time.

Example: no starting amount, 300 per month, 7% a year compounded monthly, for 30 years. You deposit 108,000 in total, yet the balance ends up several times larger because your early contributions compound for decades. The same money kept in cash would still be 108,000.

Effective annual rate and compounding frequency

Banks usually quote a nominal annual rate. When interest is added more than once a year, the real yearly return is higher than the quoted figure; this is the effective annual rate, calculated as (1 + r / n)n − 1. A nominal 5% compounded monthly is about 5.12% effective. Moving from monthly to daily compounding adds very little, while moving from yearly to monthly makes a clearer difference. When you compare accounts or deposits with different compounding schedules, the effective annual rate is the fairest number to compare.

How long does it take to double your money?

The "rule of 72" says that dividing 72 by the interest rate gives a rough doubling time: at 8% that is about 9 years. The rule is handy for low rates but drifts as rates rise. This tool skips the shortcut and calculates the exact doubling time with ln 2 / (n × ln(1 + r / n)). The doubling figure ignores monthly contributions and shows only how fast the starting amount grows on its own.

Inflation, tax and fees

The results are nominal: they show how many units of currency you will have, not what they will buy. To estimate a real return, compare the result with inflation using (1 + rate) / (1 + inflation) − 1. Interest may also be taxed, and many investment products charge management fees. The calculator does not deduct these, so for a net figure enter an after-tax, after-fee rate. Remember that rates rarely stay fixed for decades; treat long-term results as a scenario rather than a forecast.

Practical uses

  • Savings goals: Try different monthly amounts to see how quickly you can reach a house deposit, an emergency fund or a retirement target.
  • Comparing accounts: Use the effective annual rate to compare products with different compounding schedules.
  • Seeing how debt grows: Enter a balance and its annual rate to see how an unpaid debt would grow if nothing were repaid.
  • Homework and exams: Check compound interest answers you worked out by hand; the table shows each year's value step by step.

Method and limits

The calculation advances one month at a time: the balance grows by the equivalent monthly rate and then any monthly contribution is added. With no contributions this matches the standard compound interest formula exactly. The period can be up to 100 years and the rate up to 1000%. Results are rounded to two decimals for display only; intermediate steps are not rounded. Day-count conventions used by banks can create small differences, so for an exact figure rely on the statement from your provider.

Frequently asked questions

How is compound interest calculated?

With A = P × (1 + r / n) to the power of (n × t). This tool runs the calculation month by month so it can include monthly contributions as well.

What is the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is paid only on the original amount. Compound interest is added to the balance and earns interest itself, so over long periods it produces much more growth.

Is monthly or yearly compounding better?

At the same nominal rate, more frequent compounding pays more. Monthly beats yearly by a clear margin, while daily beats monthly only slightly.

What is the effective annual rate?

It is the true yearly return once compounding during the year is taken into account, calculated as (1 + r / n) to the power of n, minus 1. Use it to compare products fairly.

When are monthly contributions added?

At the end of each month; they start earning interest the following month. If you deposit at the start of the month, your real result will be about one month of interest higher.

Are tax and inflation included?

No. Results are before tax and in nominal terms. Enter an after-tax rate for a net figure and compare the result with inflation for a real return.

Is the rule of 72 accurate?

It is a good shortcut at low rates but becomes less accurate as rates rise. This tool calculates the exact doubling time with logarithms.

Is my data sent anywhere?

No. Everything is calculated in your browser; your numbers are not sent to a server or stored, and the page has no cookies or tracking code.
