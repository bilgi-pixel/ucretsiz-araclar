What is basal metabolic rate?

Basal metabolic rate is the energy your body uses at complete rest just to stay alive: keeping your heart beating, breathing, maintaining body temperature, running your brain and repairing cells. For most people it accounts for more than half of all the calories they burn in a day; the rest goes on walking, work, exercise and digesting food.

BMR varies from person to person. People with more muscle, taller people and younger people usually burn more. As we age, muscle mass tends to fall and BMR slowly declines with it. Genetics, hormones (especially thyroid), sleep and illness also play a part. A calculator cannot measure all of that; it gives an average estimate from weight, height, age and sex.

Which equation is used?

The main result uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation (1990), which is the one most often recommended today: for men BMR = 10 × weight in kg + 6.25 × height in cm − 5 × age + 5; for women the final +5 becomes −161. A 30-year-old man who weighs 80 kg and is 180 cm tall gets 800 + 1,125 − 150 + 5 = 1,780 kcal. For comparison the page also shows the revised Harris-Benedict equation from 1984. The two usually land close together, with Harris-Benedict a little higher for most people. Studies in healthy adults have found Mifflin-St Jeor closer to measured values.

From BMR to daily calories

BMR alone is not your daily need, because you move during the day. That is why BMR is multiplied by an activity factor: 1.2 for sedentary, 1.375 for lightly active, 1.55 for moderately active, 1.725 for very active and 1.9 for physically demanding jobs or twice-daily training. The result, often called TDEE (total daily energy expenditure), is roughly the number of calories that keeps your weight stable. The man in the example above, if moderately active, needs about 1,780 × 1.55 ≈ 2,759 kcal a day.

Targets for losing or gaining weight

A common rule of thumb is that losing about 0.5 kg (1 lb) of fat takes a deficit of roughly 3,500 kcal, or about 500 kcal a day below your maintenance level. The calculator shows that figure but never drops it below your BMR, since eating less than that for long periods can cost muscle, leave you tired and slow your metabolism. For gaining weight, adding around 300 kcal to maintenance is a moderate starting point that favours muscle over fat. Bodies respond differently, so track your weight for two or three weeks and adjust.

Reading the results sensibly

  • It is an estimate: Two people with the same weight and height can differ by 10% or more. Indirect calorimetry in a clinic gives the most accurate figure.
  • Muscle is not measured: The equations may underestimate needs for very muscular athletes and slightly overestimate for people with higher body fat.
  • Not for children: These equations were developed for adults; growing children and teenagers have different needs, so the tool does not accept ages under 15.
  • Special situations: Pregnancy, breastfeeding, thyroid conditions, diabetes and some medicines change energy needs. Plan your diet with a professional in these cases.

Habits that affect metabolism

You cannot change your BMR dramatically in a short time, but some habits help over the long run. Regular resistance training preserves and builds muscle, which burns more energy at rest than fat. Eating enough protein, sleeping well and avoiding very low-calorie diets also keep your metabolism from slowing unnecessarily. Be sceptical of products that promise to "boost metabolism".

Frequently asked questions

How is BMR calculated?

Most often with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation: for men 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5; for women the +5 becomes −161.

Is BMR the same as daily calorie needs?

No. BMR is only the energy used at rest. Daily needs (TDEE) are BMR multiplied by an activity factor between 1.2 and 1.9.

Which activity level should I choose?

Sedentary if you sit most of the day and do not exercise, lightly active for 1–3 workouts a week, moderately active for 3–5. If unsure, the lower level is usually more realistic.

Is Mifflin-St Jeor or Harris-Benedict more accurate?

Studies in healthy adults found Mifflin-St Jeor closer to measured values. Harris-Benedict tends to give slightly higher numbers; this tool shows both.

How many calories should I eat to lose weight?

A common starting point is about 500 kcal below your daily needs. The tool never shows a target below your BMR. For a personal plan, ask a dietitian.

Does metabolism slow down with age?

Usually yes, mainly because muscle mass declines. The equations include age for this reason, and regular strength training can slow the drop.

Can I use pounds and inches?

Yes. Switch the units to imperial and enter weight in pounds and total height in inches; the values are converted to metric before calculating.

Is my data saved or sent anywhere?

No. The calculation happens entirely in your browser; your weight, height and age are not sent to a server or stored.
