The eight phases of the moon

The moon does not make its own light. We see the part of its surface that faces the sun, and as the moon orbits Earth about once every 29.5 days, the lit portion that faces us changes. That cycle, from one new moon to the next, is called a synodic month or lunation. Astronomers divide it into four principal phases, and the calculator names eight stages so you can describe any day:

  • New Moon – the moon sits between Earth and the sun, and the side facing us is dark. It is essentially invisible.
  • Waxing Crescent – a thin sliver appears in the evening sky after sunset and grows each night.
  • First Quarter – half of the visible disc is lit, about a week after the new moon. It is highest around sunset and sets near midnight.
  • Waxing Gibbous – more than half is lit and growing toward full.
  • Full Moon – Earth is between the sun and the moon, so the whole face is lit. It rises at sunset and sets at sunrise.
  • Waning Gibbous – the lit part begins to shrink; the moon rises after dark.
  • Last Quarter (also called third quarter) – half is lit again, on the opposite side, and the moon is up in the morning sky.
  • Waning Crescent – a thin sliver is visible before sunrise until the next new moon.

“Waxing” means growing and “waning” means shrinking. “Gibbous” describes any phase with more than half of the disc lit. The quarter phases are named for the fraction of the orbit completed, not for how much is lit: at first and last quarter exactly half of the visible disc is illuminated.

Illumination and moon age explained

Illumination is the percentage of the visible disc that is lit by the sun. It is 0 percent at new moon, about 50 percent at the quarters and 100 percent at full moon. It changes fastest around the quarters and slowest near new and full moon, which is why a moon that looks “full” can really be 98 percent lit for a day or two on either side.

Moon age is the number of days since the previous new moon. Because the synodic month averages 29.53 days but varies by several hours, the age at full moon is usually between 14 and 15.5 days. The page shows the previous new moon in your time zone so you can see exactly where the current lunation began.

How the dates are calculated

The tool uses the astronomical algorithm published by Jean Meeus in Astronomical Algorithms, the reference book many almanacs rely on. It computes the exact moment of each new moon, first quarter, full moon and last quarter from the orbits of the sun and moon, including the main periodic corrections. Between those moments the illumination is interpolated smoothly. Principal phase times are accurate to within a few minutes, which is more than enough for planning, photography and curiosity. Everything runs in your browser, so the calculation also works offline.

The conversion to your time zone uses your browser’s built-in time zone database, so results shift correctly when daylight saving time starts or ends. For example, a full moon at 11:38 UTC on March 3, 2026 appears as 6:38 AM Eastern Standard Time, while the last quarter eight days later appears in Eastern Daylight Time because clocks changed in between.

Why the same phase looks different around the world

Everyone on Earth sees the same phase on the same day, but the orientation of the lit part depends on where you stand. In the northern hemisphere, including the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom, a waxing moon is lit on the right side. In the southern hemisphere the moon appears turned upside down, so the lit side is on the left. The icons on this page follow the northern hemisphere view. The date can also differ: a full moon at 2 AM UTC is still the previous evening in California, so choose your own time zone to see the date you will actually experience locally.

Practical uses for moon phase data

  • Stargazing and astrophotography. Faint galaxies and the Milky Way are best seen around the new moon, when the sky is darkest. Plan sessions for the days surrounding it.
  • Moonlit photography. A full moon rising at sunset gives dramatic landscape shots. Check the full moon time and then use a sunrise and sunset almanac for the exact rise.
  • Camping, hiking and night driving. A bright moon makes trails easier to follow; a new moon calls for headlamps.
  • Fishing, gardening and traditions. Many anglers and gardeners track the lunar calendar. Whether the moon affects results is debated, but the dates are easy to look up here.
  • Events and dates. Find out what the moon looked like on a birthday, an anniversary or a historic day back to 1900.

Moon phases also help with observing eclipses. A solar eclipse can happen only at a new moon and a lunar eclipse only at a full moon, although most months pass without one because the moon’s orbit is tilted about five degrees relative to Earth’s. Accuracy and limits

Principal phase times are accurate to a few minutes for dates between 1900 and 2100. The percentage of illumination and the intermediate phase name are derived values and are not meant for navigation or scientific publication. The tool shows the geometry of the moon, not whether you can see it; clouds, local horizon and moonrise times are not included. If you need moonrise and moonset times for your exact latitude, consult an almanac or an astronomy app for your location.

Private by design

All calculations run locally in your browser. The dates you enter are never uploaded or stored, there is no account and no location request, and the page keeps working offline once it has loaded.

Frequently asked questions

What is the moon phase today?

Press the Now button. The tool takes the current date and time in the selected time zone and shows the phase name, the moon icon, the illumination percentage and the moon age. The page opens on today’s date by default.

When is the next full moon?

Calculate any date, including today, and read the Next full moon row. It shows the exact day and time in your chosen time zone, for example Tue, Mar 3, 2026, 6:38 AM EST. Full moons occur about every 29.5 days.

How many days are there between moon phases?

The four principal phases are on average about 7.38 days apart, and a full cycle from new moon to new moon is 29.53 days. The real gap varies by up to about 14 hours because the moon’s orbit is slightly elliptical.

What does percentage illuminated mean?

It is the fraction of the visible moon disc lit by the sun. It is 0 percent at new moon, 50 percent at the first and last quarters and 100 percent at full moon. It is not a linear change; the crescent grows slowly at first and quickly around the quarters.

What is the difference between waxing and waning?

Waxing means the lit portion is growing, from new moon toward full moon. Waning means it is shrinking, from full moon toward the next new moon. In the northern hemisphere a waxing moon is lit on its right side.

What is a blue moon or a supermoon?

A blue moon is a second full moon in one calendar month, which happens about once every two to three years. A supermoon is a full or new moon near the closest point of its orbit to Earth. This calculator reports phases only and does not flag either event.

Why does the full moon date differ from other websites?

Websites may show the date in UTC, in a different time zone or round to the nearest day. Pick your own time zone here to see the local date. A full moon late in the evening in New York can already fall on the next calendar day in London.

How accurate is the calculator?

Principal phase times are accurate to within a few minutes for years 1900 through 2100, using the algorithm from Jean Meeus. Illumination between those moments is an interpolation, accurate enough for everyday use.

Does the moon look the same in the southern hemisphere?

The phase is the same on the same day, but the moon appears rotated, so the lit side is on the opposite side compared with the northern hemisphere. The icons here use the northern view.

Are my dates uploaded anywhere?

No. Everything runs in your browser. Nothing is sent, stored or tracked, and no location is requested.
